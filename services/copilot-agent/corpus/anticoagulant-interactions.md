## Summary

Anticoagulants (e.g. warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban) and antiplatelet
agents (e.g. aspirin, clopidogrel) reduce clot formation, so combining any
two of them — or combining one with another bleeding-risk drug — generally
raises bleeding risk and warrants an explicit risk/benefit conversation.
This is an educational summary for retrieval testing, not clinical advice.

## Warfarin-Specific Cautions

Warfarin's effect is sensitive to many interacting drugs and to vitamin K
intake. Common interaction cautions include: NSAIDs and other antiplatelet
agents (see `nsaid-interactions`), certain antibiotics (e.g. some
fluoroquinolones, metronidazole, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) which can
potentiate warfarin's effect, and amiodarone, which can meaningfully raise
INR. Any new interacting medication started alongside warfarin generally
warrants closer INR monitoring for a period after the change.

## Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs)

DOACs (e.g. apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran) have fewer dietary
interactions than warfarin but still carry bleeding-risk interactions with
NSAIDs, antiplatelet agents, and strong inhibitors of the metabolic/
transport pathways involved in their clearance (e.g. certain antifungals,
some antiretrovirals) — these can raise DOAC levels and bleeding risk.

## Dual Antiplatelet Therapy

Combining aspirin with a second antiplatelet agent (e.g. clopidogrel) is a
deliberate, time-limited therapy in specific clinical situations (e.g. after
a coronary stent) — it is not an incidental combination, and its bleeding
risk is generally accepted as a tradeoff during that defined window, with
duration reassessed at follow-up.

## General Practice

Before adding any new medication to a patient on an anticoagulant or
antiplatelet agent, checking the active medication list for one of these
interaction categories is a standard medication-safety step, not an
optional one.
